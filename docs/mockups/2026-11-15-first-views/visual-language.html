DayTrader · mock-up · shared visual language

One language for every tool

Five things appear on every DayTrader screen, always drawn the same way: how fast a signal changes, how old the data is, how sure we are, what could fool you, and whether a profit is certain or only probable.

The rules

  1. Solid means counted, dashed means estimated, hatched means missing. In text, an estimate gets "≈", a range and a dotted underline. In charts, estimates are dashed outlines, fans or dots, never a solid bar.
  2. Estimates are ranges, never single numbers, and never more than two significant figures ("≈ 1,500–3,000 a day", not "2,214").
  3. Colour never works alone. Every coloured state also has a word and a shape, so it reads in grey and for colour-blind players.
  4. Each colour has one job. Green/red: money in or out, good or bad. Gold: caution (and the brand accent, as in the prototype). Purple: data series and signal speed. Faction colours: houses only.
  5. Below 5 silver, no percentages. Show gold per trade instead; 1-copper price steps make % moves meaningless.
  6. One money unit per comparison (25.0 s against 17.0 s, not 0.25 g against 17 s). Totals in gold.
  7. Other houses are always labelled "reference only" until #13 confirms what a player can reach.

2. Data age

Measured from Blizzard's snapshot time (:17), not from when we fetched it, because that's when the market looked like this. Three states; the row itself changes, not just the label.

Missed hours inside a history are hatched on every chart, and lines break across them instead of guessing. This follows the Strategist's suggested default for old deals (show with age, grey out after two missed refreshes).

3. Confidence, and firm versus estimate

Confidence describes the evidence, not the deal, so it's drawn in neutral ink, never green or red. Players see three steps plus the reasons; advisors get the 0–1 number and the same reasons.

Probabilities are shown as counts of dots ("4–7 in 10"), solid for the sure part and hatched for the uncertain part. Counting dots is easier and more accurate for most people than reading a curve or a percentage (Kay et al., CHI 2016).

4. Hazard flags

Trap red, triangle: the item leaves the ranked list and moves to "Looks like a deal, isn't", with the naive number crossed out. Caution gold, circle: it stays, ranked lower, with the reason. The player's risk setting can move a flag between the two.

5. Certain versus probable

Never in the same list. Certain sits on top with a solid green edge and a single number; probable sits below with a dashed purple edge and a range. "Certain" is about the profit, not about the listing still being there, so certain rows still carry a speed tag and survival odds.
